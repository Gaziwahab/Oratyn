# Product Strategy

## Positioning

**For CS students and early-career developers who know concepts but need practice thinking and communicating like engineers, this platform provides structured, realistic engineering discussions with evidence-based feedback and a path to improve.**

## The first wedge

Start with learners preparing for internships and entry-level software roles. Their needs are concrete: explain a solution, discuss data structures and systems, review code, reason about trade-offs, and communicate clearly in technical rounds and team settings.

Avoid marketing the first release as a complete platform for every developer at every experience level. Expand only after the core audience repeatedly uses the product and demonstrates value.

## Differentiation

The opportunity is not that no adjacent products exist. Adjacent products already serve developer publishing, structured debate, coding practice, learning roadmaps, and interview preparation. The intended differentiation is the combination of:

1. Small-group engineering discussion rather than passive content or isolated coding.
2. A repeatable protocol that includes opening reasoning, cross-questioning, revision, and reflection.
3. Realistic engineering prompts with changing constraints over time.
4. Individual feedback grounded in stored contribution evidence.
5. A competency model that recommends what to practise next.

This combination is a hypothesis to validate, not a proven market moat.

## Value propositions

### Learner

- Practise explaining technical ideas without waiting for an interview.
- Discover gaps in reasoning and understanding.
- Learn to ask better questions and evaluate alternatives.
- Build a record of demonstrated progress.

### Mentor or educator (later)

- Run structured sessions with clear learning objectives.
- See what participants can explain and where they need help.
- Reuse scenarios and rubrics.

### Developer community (later)

- Build meaningful technical relationships through doing work together rather than feed engagement.

## Habit and retention hypothesis

A sustainable habit should be built around a useful weekly practice rhythm, not infinite scrolling. The first hypothesis is that users who complete one or two quality sessions per week and receive specific next steps will return more often than users who only browse topics.

Do not add streak pressure or notifications that shame users. Reminders should be opt-in and easy to disable.

## Validation questions

1. Do learners actually want group discussion, or do they primarily want one-to-one practice?
2. What room format produces the most useful learning: design review, debugging, teach-back, debate, or code review?
3. Can AI reports identify specific, actionable improvements users agree with?
4. Can a small pilot maintain enough participation without automated matching?
5. Does a competency roadmap make users more likely to practise again?
6. Will users return without a public leaderboard or content feed?

## Validation before scaling

Run a small pilot with a curated topic set and a human facilitator. Observe completed sessions, no-show rates, participant contribution distribution, report usefulness, and repeat practice. Interview participants after multiple sessions. Do not treat sign-ups or time-on-platform as product-market fit.

## Go/no-go signals for expanding beyond MVP

Proceed to voice, automated matching, richer AI practice, or complex simulations only when the current text-based loop demonstrates:

- Users can find or schedule a session reliably.
- Sessions finish and produce usable feedback.
- Participants perceive the feedback as fair and actionable.
- At least a meaningful cohort returns for another practice session.
- Moderation and privacy controls work under real usage.
